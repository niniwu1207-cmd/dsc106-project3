How Do Warming Patterns Vary Across Latitudes and Over Time?

Warming since each latitude's 1850–1879 average (°C), averaged over longitude and grouped by decade.

Drag on the heatmap to pick a time span: top = start, bottom = end.

Data: CMIP6 NASA-GISS GISS-E2-1-G historical run (r1i1p1f1), monthly near-surface air temperature. 2010–2014 is a partial decade.

Motivation

Global warming does not affect all regions equally. In our exploratory analysis of CMIP6 data, the Arctic warmed about 1.6°C between 1850–1879 and 1985–2014, more than twice as much as the tropics. Comparing decades also showed that this gap was not always there. So we asked: How do warming patterns vary across latitudes and over time? More specifically: Is the Arctic warming faster than the rest of the world, and since when?

Data and Transformations

We used one simulation: the NASA-GISS GISS-E2-1-G model, CMIP6 historical experiment, ensemble member r1i1p1f1, monthly near-surface air temperature from 1850 to 2014. We converted temperature from Kelvin to Celsius, averaged over all longitudes so each 2° latitude band has one value, grouped the months into decades (2010–2014 only has five years), and subtracted each latitude's own 1850–1879 average. We used temperature anomalies rather than absolute temperatures because absolute temperature is dominated by latitude (about −40°C near the South Pole versus +27°C at the equator), which hides changes of less than 2°C. Using the same historical baseline also lets readers compare decades consistently. The Arctic and global averages shown under the chart are area-weighted by cos(latitude), because a band near the pole covers much less of the Earth than a band near the equator.

Design Rationale

Line chart. We used a line chart because latitude is a continuous variable, so readers can follow how warming changes from the Southern Hemisphere to the Northern Hemisphere. Both latitude and warming are encoded by position, the most accurate visual channel. The chart shows two decades: the start (blue) and end (red) of the chosen time span. The area between the lines is shaded red where the later decade is warmer and blue where it is cooler, so the change itself is visible, not just two separate curves. A thick marker labels the latitude with the largest change. Below the chart, one line compares how much the Arctic changed with the whole globe.

Heatmap. Our data has three variables (latitude, decade and temperature anomaly), so we added a latitude-by-decade heatmap: latitude on the x-axis, decade on the y-axis and anomaly as color. It gives an overview of all 17 decades at once, which two lines cannot. We used a diverging blue–white–red scale centered at 0°C because the data has a meaningful midpoint (no change) and some latitudes and decades were cooler than the baseline. The star (★) marks the first decade in which the Arctic warmed more than twice the global average. We only count decades where global warming is above 0.2°C, so tiny early values do not create misleading ratios.

Linking the two views. The heatmap uses the same latitude axis as the line chart and sits directly below it, so a column in the heatmap lines up with the same latitude on the chart. Hovering on either view marks that latitude on both.

Color. Blue always means the starting decade and coral red the ending decade, which matches the heatmap, where red means warmer. Light blue backgrounds mark the Arctic and Antarctic regions beyond 66.5°, to draw attention to the high latitudes without hiding the data.

Latitude axis. We drew latitude in equal degrees. This makes the polar regions look larger than they really are: the area north of 66.5°N is only about 4% of the Earth's surface. We kept equal degrees because they are easier to read, and we account for area in the Arctic and global averages by weighting by cos(latitude).

Interaction Design

Brushing. Users drag on the heatmap to choose a time span. The top of the box is the starting decade and the bottom is the ending decade. The box can be dragged to slide a span through time, and clicking a row moves the nearer end of the box. Rows outside the span are dimmed. Play keeps the starting decade and moves the ending decade forward to 2014 one decade at a time, so users can watch the gap grow. Tooltips on the line chart snap to the nearest latitude and show both decades' values and their difference. Tooltips on the heatmap show the value of that cell.

These interactions reveal patterns a single static chart does not. Different time spans have very different shapes. For example, from the 1950s to the 1970s, northern mid-latitudes (30–60°N) actually cooled by about 0.1–0.25°C. After the 1980s, the Arctic pulls far ahead of every other latitude.

Alternatives We Considered

  • A world map of temperature change. We tried this during exploration. It is visually striking, but our question is about latitude and time, and a map can only show one period at a time.
  • Drawing all decades as lines. Seventeen overlapping lines were impossible to tell apart. The heatmap shows every decade without overlap.
  • Two dropdown menus. Our first version used two dropdown menus to pick the decades. They hid the overview and made trying many comparisons slow, so we replaced them with brushing directly on the heatmap.

Limitations. This is a single run of one climate model, not observations, so other models would give somewhat different numbers. The edge latitudes near ±89° cover very little area and are noisier, so the largest change sometimes appears at the poles.

Development Process

We started by exploring the dataset in Google Colab and discussing possible research questions, then decided together which direction to focus on. Hsin Yu and Jay worked on building the heatmap, while Yi-Ning built the line chart. Throughout the process, all three members discussed the design, reviewed the visualization, worked on the write-up, and suggested changes to improve the final result.

In total, we spent approximately 15 person-hours developing this project. The most time-consuming parts were exploring the dataset, deciding how to present the comparison between decades (we went from dropdown menus to brushing on the heatmap), and getting the linked hover, brush and Play button to work together.
